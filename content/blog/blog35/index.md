There is something wonderfully vulnerable about inviting someone into your everyday life.

Not the version of yourself that exists on vacation or during special events, but the ordinary one. The one that knows which exit to take without thinking, where to stop for coffee, which friends always seem to end up around the same table, and what a quiet Tuesday evening looks like. Those things rarely feel remarkable while you're living them. They're simply your life. It often takes seeing them through someone else's eyes to realize how much of yourself lives inside those ordinary moments.

For the last nine months, that's exactly what Kuma has been doing for me.

Every few weeks I'd board another overnight flight to London, usually carrying enough over-the-counter medicine to stock a small pharmacy. Tums, Imodium, Benadryl, ibuprofen, allergy medicine—somewhere along the way it became tradition that I would arrive with a fresh American care package for the house. By Monday morning I'd be making the familiar journey toward Worcester Park, already slipping back into a rhythm that somehow became comfortable much faster than I ever expected.

Those visits were never really about London.

The city certainly became familiar. I found favorite restaurants. I stopped having to think about which Underground line to take. Friends became people I hugged instead of people I introduced myself to. Collared stopped feeling like somewhere I visited and started feeling like somewhere I belonged.

But London was never the point.

The point was that Kuma opened his world to me.

He introduced me to the people who matter most to him. I learned the rhythms of the house, shared dinners around the table, celebrated birthdays, spent evenings with friends, and slowly discovered the little routines that no visitor would ever stumble across on their own. I wasn't simply visiting another country. I was being welcomed into a life that existed long before I arrived, and somewhere along the way those few precious days stopped feeling like trips and started feeling like returning.

Eventually, every visit reached the same quiet ending.

We'd leave the house together and walk to the SL7 stop in Worcester Park. We'd stand there talking for just a few more minutes, both of us pretending we weren't watching for the bus to appear around the corner. When it finally arrived, there would always be one last hug before I climbed aboard. I'd find a seat by the window, look back toward the stop, and watch Kuma grow smaller until the bus disappeared around the bend.

It was hard not to feel a little sad every single time that bus pulled away.

I wasn't the only one who felt it. Before I was even halfway to Heathrow, our Telegram chats would already be lighting up.

> "I already miss you."

> "Miss you too."

The conversation never really stopped. It simply moved from standing beside each other at the bus stop to messages from the back of a London bus, then from Heathrow, and eventually from somewhere over the Atlantic. Over the next few hours—and usually the next few days—we'd keep chatting as though neither of us was quite ready to admit the visit had ended. It became its own little ritual, a gentle way of stretching goodbye just a little longer.

That little bus stop became the punctuation mark at the end of every chapter.

Except this one.

This trip looks different before it even begins.

Instead of packing a suitcase for another visit, I'm carrying a backpack. One change of clothes, my passport, the usual pharmacy delivery for the house, and the essentials. I land on Monday morning, we'll spend one evening together, and before we know it Tuesday morning will arrive.

We'll leave the house and make that familiar walk to the SL7 stop once again. We'll stand there waiting for the same bus we've waited for so many times before. Only this time, when it pulls up to the curb, there won't be one last hug before we part ways.

Instead, I'll lift Kuma's suitcase onto the bus.

We'll both climb aboard.

We'll find a pair of seats together and watch the neighborhood disappear through the window side by side.

The journey will begin exactly where every goodbye has begun before, only this time the bus won't be carrying me away from Kuma. It'll be carrying both of us toward Heathrow, toward Atlanta, and toward the beginning of a completely different adventure.

I've caught myself smiling every time I picture that moment.

Not because it's dramatic, but because it's wonderfully ordinary. It's the same bus. The same route. The same stop where we've said goodbye so many times before. The only thing that's changing is that, for once, neither of us will be left standing behind. Somehow that tiny difference feels bigger than crossing an ocean.

Kuma has jokingly started referring to the trip as "Kuma & Ruff Do America," and honestly, I don't think there could be a better title.

There are certainly some big moments on the calendar. We'll spend time with friends, make our way to Augusta for the Georgia Pet and Handler Contest, and explore Atlanta together. But what makes me smile the most are the wonderfully ordinary things he's excited about.

He's fascinated by Walmart because it's one of those places that seems to exist in every American movie, television show, and internet meme. He wants to experience Buc-ee's because somehow Americans have convinced the rest of the world that a gas station can become a destination. He wants breakfast at Denny's, wants to find out what Southern grits actually taste like, and has become genuinely curious about root beer simply because it's one of those unmistakably American things he's seen referenced throughout pop culture.

None of those places are famous landmarks.

They're simply pieces of everyday America.

And I absolutely love that.

He's not coming here to check famous attractions off a list. He's coming because he wants to understand the place that shaped me. He's curious about the little things that make up my everyday life, the places I'd probably drive past without giving them a second thought simply because they've always been there.

I'm also quietly hoping one more thing works out, although that's entirely up to the non-rev gods.

Thanks to my job, I've been incredibly fortunate over the years. More often than not, I've been able to cross the Atlantic in Delta One. Somewhere along the way, lie-flat seats, decent meals, and actually getting a little sleep before landing in London became part of my routine. It's one of those experiences I've caught myself taking for granted simply because I've been lucky enough to enjoy it so often.

Kuma hasn't.

If everything lines up and the upgrade fairy smiles on us, this will be his first time experiencing a premium cabin. I honestly think I'll spend more time watching his reactions than paying attention to my own. Seeing someone discover they can actually stretch out and sleep on an overnight flight, watching them press every seat control like a kid with a new toy, and seeing that smile when they realize how different the journey can feel... I think that'll be one of my favorite moments of the trip.

The funny thing is, that's become the unexpected theme of this entire adventure.

Watching someone experience the familiar has a way of making it feel brand new again.

Kuma already knows so much about my life, but mostly through hundreds of little moments shared through Telegram. He knows the names because they've appeared in stories after Pup Night. He knows Harry because there have been countless pictures of a corgi firmly convinced he's the center of the universe. He's followed along as I've prepared for Georgia Pet and Handler, celebrated teaching opportunities, worried about travel, written blog posts, and shared the little victories and frustrations that make up an ordinary week. Piece by piece, message by message, he's built a picture of my world without ever having set foot in it.

Now those stories get to become places.

The names become people he'll finally meet.

The pictures become neighborhoods we'll walk together.

The conversations become memories we'll create side by side.

I'm excited to introduce him to the people who've become my chosen family. So many of them know Kuma only through stories and photos. They've celebrated our milestones from afar, checked in on him after his surgery, listened to me enthusiastically recount another trip to London, and asked more than once, "So when do we finally get to meet him?"

Now they will.

I'm excited to watch him experience Pup Night, to introduce him to the wonderfully chaotic energy that makes Atlanta feel like home, and to watch two parts of my life that have existed separately for months finally come together in the same room.

I'm excited for him to meet Harry, who I'm fairly certain will spend the first five minutes deciding whether Kuma throws a tennis ball correctly.

I'm excited for him to spend time with my parents. Over the past year they've gotten to know the most authentic version of me, and my dad recently told me he felt like he'd gotten his son back. There aren't many things a parent could say that mean more than that. I'm looking forward to sharing quiet dinners, conversations around the table, and all the ordinary moments that build real relationships.

Then we'll head to Augusta.

For a long time I thought the Georgia Pet and Handler Contest was the reason for this trip. I've poured my heart into preparing for it, and having Kuma there means more than I can adequately explain.

But somewhere along the way I realized the contest isn't the destination.

It's another opportunity for Kuma to understand my world.

He'll meet the people who encouraged me to run. He'll experience the warmth, encouragement, laughter, nervous energy, and joy that make this pet play family so special to me. Whether I come home with a title or simply another unforgettable weekend of memories, he'll understand why this corner of my life has become so important.

As I write this, my backpack is sitting by the door.

It's the smallest bag I've ever packed for a trip to London.

Because, in truth, I'm not really flying to London.

I'm flying to pick up Kuma.

Over the last nine months, he opened the door to his world without hesitation. He let me experience his everyday life exactly as it was, and in doing so gave me one of the greatest gifts I've ever received.

Now it's my turn.

Maybe that's what love really does.

It reminds you that the things you've stopped noticing are often extraordinary to someone seeing them for the first time. A suburban bus stop. A lie-flat seat somewhere over the Atlantic. A Buc-ee's the size of a supermarket. A plate of Southern grits. A room full of friends who are about to become someone else's friends too.

Over the next two weeks, I have a feeling I'll spend just as much time watching Kuma discover my world as I will showing it to him.

And somewhere between Worcester Park and Atlanta, between everyday moments and unforgettable memories, I think we'll both end up discovering something new.

Not just about America.

About home.

Because for the first time, when the SL7 pulls away from that little bus stop, neither of us will be watching the other disappear.

We'll both be on board.
