It’s National Walk Your Dog Week, so I thought it apropos to talk about the puppy walk. As the pup, pet, and handler scene has gained visibility in the last several years, social media accounts are getting flooded with a particular sub-event at kink events around the globe: the pup walk.

What is a pup walk? A pup walk is a celebration of community and the joy that is pet play. It’s an organized walk through the city, with participants in gear being silly, stopping for pictures, and doing what pets do best: playing. From Folsom Street East to Darklands and Pup Out! London, more and more large events are starting to organize pup walks for attendees.

There is a certain magic in moving as a pack. Barking, tails, hoods, flags, spontaneous playfulness, and a shared headspace can really be an experience. One pup in a hood may attract a curious glance. A pack of pups and handlers—with ears up, tails wagging, and handlers roughly keeping everyone pointed in the same direction—creates an unmistakable sense of presence. What might make a small group of newer pups feel vulnerable and alone now becomes an empowering experience where you can be yourself, make new friends, and create lasting memories with dozens, if not hundreds, of others.

Every pup brings a different energy to the collective pack. Some bounce from pup to pup, eager to greet the entire pack. Others remain close to a trusted friend or smaller group. There are confident pups, mischievous, chaotic pups, shy pups finding their footing, and experienced community members quietly watching out for everyone around them. No one has to be a particular “pup” or pet to participate. There is no wrong way to join in the fun.

The variety is a part of that magic. A puppy walk also creates a kind of social permission. Barking, wagging, playful posing, and other expressions of pet headspace can feel easier when they are reflected in others around you. Newer pups don’t have to carry the pressure of “doing puppy right” or being the only person in or out of gear. Experienced pups can be the welcoming presence a lot of newcomers seek out.

Handlers also play an important role and contribute in their own way by keeping pups on the pre-planned route, making sure everyone has treats and water, and carrying gear that may become too cumbersome for a pup eagerly bouncing around the parade. The pack in a pup walk is not defined; there is no hierarchical structure. You can come with your own pack or come solo, just looking to make connections.

Then there is the visibility aspect. A large group of pups moving through a public space can make even the most vanilla of folks crack a smile. It’s difficult to overlook, and that’s the point. The walk allows a community that is often encountered in private spaces or only online to appear together in person in daylight. It says that pup play is not always about sex, gear, or individual headspace. It’s about friendship, creativity, expression, and belonging. Those things are important.

That public visibility also comes with a bit of responsibility. People watching have not consented to become part of anyone’s play, so the group needs to ensure that it respects event rules, personal boundaries, photography preferences, and the boundaries of the local cities and surrounding community. That awareness doesn’t diminish the fun; rather, it ensures that we can show the world that pups can exist and behave and that pup walks will be welcomed at other events for years to come.

For new pets and handlers, these events are a great introduction to the world of pet play. Sometimes a newcomer can feel intimidated. Nighttime events may not feel comfortable or may feel a bit intense if you don’t know the people. Some pups may not be into the sexual aspect of a playspace, and so it can feel really intimidating. A pup walk, on the other hand, offers a more familiar structure: arrive, meet the group, pose for a photo, and move together.

There are many ways to participate. Pup or pet gear is never required. Pups, pets, handlers, friends, photographers, or curious community members are welcome to participate. Of course, it’s always encouraged to observe before deciding whether to be more involved. There are plenty of pup walk videos out on the internet that give some flavor of what to expect. Gear is never the price of admission. Custom hoods, tails, or fetish wear can be fun, but they absolutely do not make the pet or pup. The only gear required is comfortable shoes for walking on potentially uneven surfaces and a water bottle to stay hydrated.

A puppy walk can create natural conversations that feel less formal, and new pups are free to ask questions in a less formal setting. Shared movement gives people something to do when they are unsure what to say. A puppy walk can open the door, but there is no expectation of what happens on the other side.

Each walk has a different vibe and feeling. Folsom Street East, for example, has the energy of a street festival. There is a large presence of leather and kink, and it feels very festival-like. Darklands has a walk that’s more a part of the structured programming. Instead of being squeezed in between events, the Darklands pup walk is part of the main event. Darklands also includes Camp K9, PupSoccer, socials, and the European Puppy and Handler Contest. Pup Out! London and Puppy Pride are smaller, more regular local events that provide some of the more continuous social interaction and local friendships, although pups from around the world still occasionally attend. All of these events share that playful pack energy and are chock-full of opportunities to meet people beyond one’s existing circle. None of these events are the definitive pup walk; all bring different and unique experiences to the table, and I would encourage you to try several if possible. Do plan on learning more about the cultures and rules of the event so you know what to plan for.

None of these events happen spontaneously and do require a bit of planning. A little preparation on the attendees’ part can go a long way in ensuring everyone has a safe and playful time. Before the event, double-check if registration is required or encouraged. This helps organizers plan for crowds, venue capacity, or safety concerns. Also, be sure to pack some extra water and any medications you may need. Check the weather and plan for unexpected rain, heat, or cold. It’s also a great idea to have something to eat before the event, as you may not have the opportunity to stop on the route to grab something. Don’t forget that hoods, rubber, neoprene, and other gear can retain heat.

Before the paws hit the pavement, remember to check that you have your hoods, collars, leashes, and tails packed with you. Secure anything that could fall off in a crowd. The last thing you want is to lose a piece of gear that is expensive or has sentimental value to you and lessen your experience. Also, avoid gear or equipment that restricts breathing, visibility, hearing, or movement. Be aware of where you might be able to stash excess gear, bags, or streetwear.

It should also be noted that these are very photographic events. It’s important to remember that just being in public doesn’t eliminate the value of asking permission. Get consent before posting close-up images or tagging someone without their express permission. Be mindful when capturing photos of pups not in hoods. Not everyone wants to be an Instagram star, so again, it’s important to always ask permission. Also, do not assume that all pups want to interact physically. It’s fun to give hugs and scratches, but it’s important to get consent before engaging with more than a paw wave.

The relationships that you make at a pup walk can last lifetimes. You’ll make connections across the globe. You may choose to return the following year or to become an event volunteer or organizer. One pet walk can become an entry point into years of community involvement and friendships.

At the end of the day, a pup walk is about much more than putting on a hood, taking pictures, and being silly in public. It’s about finding community and realizing that you are part of something larger. You may arrive knowing no one and leave with new friends. You may find a local pack, attend another event, or even decide to volunteer or organize a walk yourself.

So, if you get the opportunity to join a pup walk, give it a try. You don’t need expensive gear, a handler, or the perfect bark. Bring some water, comfortable shoes, and a willingness to meet the pack. You never know where those first few steps may lead.

Long after the ears come off and the tails are packed away, the most important part of the walk remains: the knowledge that somewhere out there, the broader pack is still moving with you.
