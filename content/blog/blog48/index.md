As I’ve become more visible in the pup, pet, and handler community, I keep hearing the same questions from newer pups: “How do I find other pups?” and “Why don’t I feel welcome in the pup community?”
That always hits me in the feels a little hard. I’ve watched this community grow over the years, and it’s helped me discover who I am and who I want to be. So when someone says they don’t feel welcome, I take it a little personally. This is a community that means a lot to me, and I want others to have the chance to find that same sense of belonging.
But I have to remind myself that their experience is not my own. Some have had a genuinely bad experience. Others don’t know where to start, or they’re waiting for someone to reach out to them. Those situations need different responses, and understanding which one we’re talking about matters.
Being excluded, feeling nervous, and not knowing anyone can all feel similar, but they call for different actions. I often ask what made someone feel unwelcome and listen to what happened. Sometimes someone was treated poorly, and that deserves to be acknowledged. Other times, they walked into a room where everyone seemed to know each other and didn’t know how to join in. In those situations, I ask whether they had a chance to introduce themselves or start a conversation.
Sometimes all it takes is a compliment on a hood or gear to get the conversation started. “Hey, I like the colors on your hood. I’m Ruff, by the way, and it’s my first time here.” That can be enough to break the ice and start getting a better feel for the event. Also, don’t stop at one person or pup. Everyone is different, so if one conversation doesn’t click, you can try another introduction while respecting whether someone is available to chat.
As an introvert myself, I know that first introduction can be hard. For me, leaning into my pup headspace can help me get out of my own way and say hello. That might help you too. You don’t have to stop being nervous before you take a small step, and every conversation gives you a little more practice. One positive experience can start to ease some of those nerves.
Finding a community takes initiative. You can’t expect it to find you. Look on social media for local PAH (pups and handlers) groups, pup events, or clubs. Check local LGBTQ+ bars and clubs for gear, leather, or pup nights. Get to know the organizers and introduce yourself. They can help you understand the event and connect with people who want to welcome newcomers.
Anxiety, distance, and accessibility can make this harder, especially for pups in rural areas. Connecting virtually can be a way to start building relationships when meeting in person isn’t possible.
It’s also important to give these relationships time. People need opportunities to get to know you, learn what your interests are, and discover what you have in common. Don’t assume that after one meeting, someone is going to be your new best friend and invite you along to everything. Be upfront. Let them know you’re new to the pup community, want to get to know it better, and would appreciate suggestions or answers to questions you may have.
Give yourself permission to find the right fit. You’re not going to have the same experience at every pup event or with every pup you encounter. That’s okay. One group or bad experience doesn’t represent the entirety of the community. Different spaces and events offer different experiences.
For example, the Georgia Kennel Club focuses on hosting events where pups, pets, and handlers 18 and over can meet in spaces that aren’t clubs or bars. We’ve hosted a Bark in the Park where pups came together and played frisbee. We’re getting ready for a puppy takeover at the Renaissance faire, as well as a community service event in December.
Bar nights can also be an option for those 21 and over who enjoy that setting. You don’t have to drink to participate, and you don’t have to enjoy bars to find your community. If you feel uncomfortable or your boundaries are crossed, it’s okay to leave and find another opportunity.
I want to touch on something else: new pups should work on building relationships before pursuing titles. I think a lot of pups new to the community feel like they immediately need to be collared or find an Alpha, pack, or handler. There’s nothing wrong with wanting those relationships. But pursuing a title before you know the person can create expectations that neither of you is ready to meet.
These relationships don’t happen instantly. You need to get to know people, see whether their values align with your own, and find out whether there’s mutual interest in building that kind of connection. These kinds of relationships take time and trust.
They also take explicit, ongoing consent. Talk about what you each want, what the roles mean, and what responsibilities you’re agreeing to. A title or collar doesn’t give blanket permission, and everyone needs room to say no, revisit an agreement, or change their mind.
If you reach that place of compatibility, keep talking. Revisit expectations, clarify roles, and keep the communication going. That’s part of building a healthy relationship.
I can say from my past relationships and the ones I have now that those dynamics evolved after I put in the time to get to know the people involved. My Alpha and I chatted for months before even meeting, and even after meeting, it was still several months before I accepted his collar. We constantly have discussions about our boundaries, what our titles mean to one another, and where we fit into the bigger picture of our relationship.
Remember that everyone gets a choice in whether and how they want that relationship to develop. Give each other room to make that choice without pressure. A collar can symbolize a relationship, but it can’t do the work of building one.
I bring this up because belonging to a community doesn’t require you to be collared or in a pack. If you feel you’re a pup, guess what? You’re a pup. You can always wear your own collar if it helps you feel connected, but you don’t need someone else’s collar to make your belonging valid.
Friendships and community connections matter in their own right. Some may develop into a pack, handler, or Alpha relationship. Others may remain friendships, and those are worth building too. Give those connections the time and space to grow into something everyone involved wants.
I truly want everyone to have a similar experience with this community to the one I’ve had. I strive to be a gateway for others to experience it. But every pup is going to have their own journey, and taking those first steps is part of it.
For those of us who have found our place, remember where you came from. Remember what you wanted when you were first starting out, and be that pup or pet for the newcomers. Be open to those pups taking the brave first step of saying hello or going to their first event. If you see a pup on the sidelines, don’t be afraid to take that first step yourself and say hello.
I can’t promise that every space will be your jam or that every pup will become your friend. What I can say is that I want you to have the chance to find what I have found. That starts with taking a brave step and giving people a chance to get to know you.
